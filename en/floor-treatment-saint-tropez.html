Floor treatment in Saint-Tropez

Eight materials, eight behaviours. What each floor fears, and what it needs.

01The diagnosis 02The eight materials 03The proof 04Habits

The diagnosis

On the Gulf, a floor ages faster than anywhere else. Three causes, always the same.

Sand

Carried in from the beaches with every step, it acts as an abrasive between sole and surface. It does not soil the floor: it sands it down, slowly, along the walking routes.

Salt

Sea air penetrates porous materials and attacks surface sealers. On a travertine or stone terrace, the effect shows first in the shaded areas, never in the dry ones.

The season

In four summer months, a shop or a villa takes a full year's footfall. On top of that, the humidity swing between July and January makes terracotta and wood move.

The instinct is to clean harder. That is almost always the mistake: an aggressive product strips the sealer before the dirt, and the floor then soils twice as fast. Treatment starts by identifying the material, its finish and its history.

The materials

What saves one floor destroys another

Marble

Porosity
Medium
Vulnerable to
Acids
Finish
Polished or honed

Marble is a limestone, so it is soft and vulnerable to acids. Lemon juice, a descaler or an ordinary household cleaner is enough to leave a dull mark that no washing will lift: the surface has been chemically etched, not soiled.

Mechanical wear adds to that. In an entrance or a corridor, microscopic sand scratches the polish and the marble dulls in patches, following the walking routes exactly.

What it needsA pH-neutral cleaner day to day, and a re-polish once the shine has gone in patches.

Travertine

Family
Limestone
Porosity
High
Vulnerable to
Standing water and chlorine
Finish
Honed, pores left open or filled

Travertine is the stone of terraces and poolsides across the Gulf. Its porosity is visible to the naked eye: those cavities give it its texture, and they hold on to everything — dust, water, chlorine, sun cream.

Outdoors it greens in the shade and hollows where the rain washes it. Indoors, unsealed travertine stains permanently under a plant pot or a furniture leg.

What it needsA sealer that penetrates without forming a film, renewed periodically, and refilling of the pores if the original finish was filled.

Terracotta and tomettes

Family
Fired clay
Porosity
Very high
Vulnerable to
Alkaline products
Finish
Waxed or oiled

This is the Provençal floor par excellence, and the most absorbent of them all. A tomette literally drinks whatever you put on it: water, oil, wax, and unsuitable maintenance products too, which build up in successive layers.

The classic outcome is a floor gone grey-brown and tacky, thought to be dirty when it is in fact saturated. No amount of extra washing will recover it: the layers have to come off before the finish can be redone.

What it needsFull removal of the old layers, a real drying time, then a waxed or matt finish depending on the look wanted.

Parquet and wood

Family
Solid or engineered wood
Porosity
Varies with the finish
Vulnerable to
Water and humidity swings
Finish
Oiled or lacquered

With parquet, everything depends on the finish, and that is the question almost nobody asks. A lacquered floor carries a film on the surface: when the film is scratched, it is the film that needs redoing, not the wood. An oiled floor has no film: it is fed, repaired in patches, and hates being lacquered.

Getting the family wrong is expensive. Oiling a lacquered floor achieves nothing, the oil cannot penetrate. Lacquering an oiled floor means sanding right back to bare wood.

What it needsIdentify the existing finish before anything else, then work within the same family — or commit to a full change.

Polished concrete

Family
Cement mortar
Porosity
Low as long as the sealer holds
Vulnerable to
Its sealer wearing through
Finish
Sealed or waxed

Polished concrete is a continuous, seamless surface protected by a sealer or a wax. As long as that protection holds, it is very easy to live with. The day it wears through, in the pivot areas and in front of thresholds, the bare substrate is exposed and stains immediately.

It is a material that gives no warning. It looks intact, then a mark appears and never leaves, because it is in the material rather than on it.

What it needsKeeping an eye on the traffic areas, and renewing the sealer before the substrate is reached.

Terrazzo and granito

Family
Cement and marble chips
Porosity
Medium
Vulnerable to
Acids and alkalis alike
Finish
Polished

Terrazzo, known as granito in older buildings, combines two materials that behave differently: marble chips sensitive to acids, set in a cement binder sensitive to water and alkalis.

That is what makes it treacherous. A product that suits the chips attacks the binder, and vice versa. On the original granito of apartment lobbies, the wrong treatment leaves a permanently pitted look.

What it needsA product compatible with both components at once, and a polish redone across the whole surface rather than in patches.

Encaustic cement tiles

Family
Unfired pigmented cement
Porosity
High, for life
Vulnerable to
Abrasive stripping
Finish
Matt, sealed

The encaustic tile is not fired. Its colour sits in a thin surface layer, a few millimetres only, and it stays porous for life. That is what gives it its patina, and it is also what makes it fragile.

Strip it too hard and the pattern goes with it. Once the design is gone, it does not come back. On the old tiles of village houses, that is the most common mistake and the most final.

What it needsGentle work, tested on a hidden area, and a sealer that lets the tile breathe.

08

Porcelain tile

Family
Pressed ceramic
Porosity
Almost none
Vulnerable to
Nothing, except its grout
Finish
Matt or polished

Porcelain is the toughest floor on this list. It fears neither acids, nor water, nor footfall. That is why it goes into shops, schools and lobbies.

Its weak point is not the tile, it is the grout. Porous and sitting below the surface, it collects all the dirt and gives the whole floor a dull grey look while the tiles themselves are untouched. The floor looks worn; it is simply outlined in grey.

What it needsWork aimed at the grout, and sealing of that grout to space out the repeats.

No two floors are washed the same way. That is the whole trade.

Habits

Four habits that damage, four that save

The wrong instinct What to do instead

Cleaning harder when the floor stays dull.

Understanding why it is dull. A scratched or etched surface is not washed, it is resurfaced.

Using a household product because it smells good and foams.

Choosing by material. An acid on marble or an alkali on terracotta does irreversible damage.

Piling on wax or sealer without ever stripping.

Removing the old before laying the new. It is the build-up that turns a floor grey and tacky.

Treating the whole floor at once, with no trial.

Testing on a hidden area, in a corner or under furniture, and looking at the result once dry.

The hidden-area test is not a precaution: it is the only way to know what a floor will give before committing the whole surface. It therefore comes before the quote, not after.

Frequently asked questions

What we are asked most often

My marble is dull in places, can it be saved?

In most cases, yes. Marble that is dull in patches is usually not dirty: its polish has been scratched by footfall or etched by an acidic product. The shine is recovered by working the surface, not by washing it more. What cannot be recovered are chips and deep breaks, which are a repair job.

Does a new floor need treating?

It depends on the material. Porcelain needs nothing, apart from its grout. New terracotta, travertine or encaustic tiles, by contrast, are at their most vulnerable: porous and unsealed. A first sealer applied before the space is used prevents permanent stains in the very first weeks.

How often should a floor be resealed?

There is no universal interval: it depends on the material, the product, the footfall and the exposure. The reliable signal is how water behaves: as long as a drop beads on the surface, the sealer is holding. When it soaks in and darkens the material, it is time.

Can you work without closing the shop?

Often yes, by working outside opening hours or in successive zones. The limiting factor is not the work itself but the drying time, which varies with the material and the product. It is planned, and it is stated up front.

What is the difference between cleaning and treating a floor?

Cleaning removes what sits on the floor. Treating acts on the floor itself: taking off the accumulated layers, resurfacing, then sealing. A floor still dirty after a thorough clean does not need more cleaning, it needs treatment.

Do you work outside Saint-Tropez?

Yes. We cover the whole Gulf of Saint-Tropez, including Ramatuelle, Gassin, Grimaud, Cogolin, Sainte-Maxime, Cavalaire-sur-Mer and La Croix-Valmer, plus other towns in the Var depending on the job.
